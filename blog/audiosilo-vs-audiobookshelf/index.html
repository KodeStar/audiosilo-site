your-server/web

Yes, AudioSilo is a good Audiobookshelf alternative if what you want is audiobooks: a server that never writes to your folders, released phone apps in the App Store and Google Play, and a player built around listening to whole books. Audiobookshelf is the better fit if you also want an ebook library in many formats, uploads and tag editing from the browser, podcasts without an extra container, single sign-on, or a choice of third-party apps. On the same low-power home server (an Intel i3-N305, a 15 W mini PC chip) and the same 1,000 to 5,000 LibriVox audiobooks, AudioSilo 2.5.0 finished its first scan 25 to 40% sooner, rescanned an unchanged library about 3 to 7 times faster, idled at 14 MiB of memory to Audiobookshelf’s roughly 50, answered nearly every request we timed sooner, and under full load from our Mac answered 6 to 7 times as many requests a second (about twice as many when the load ran on the server itself). Audiobookshelf used less CPU time to scan, was quicker to send a cover it had already resized (on the 1,000-book libraries timed from our Mac, and on all three timed on the server itself), and was slightly quicker at one admin request at 5,000 books.

Audiobookshelf is a great project. It’s mature, it has years of releases behind it, most self-hosting guides recommend it first, and a whole family of third-party apps is built on it. We make AudioSilo, so read this with that in mind: we’ve tried to be fair, every number below comes from a harness you can download and run yourself, and we show the results where Audiobookshelf comes out ahead as well as where AudioSilo does.

What Audiobookshelf does that AudioSilo doesn’t

  • Ebooks as a library of their own. Audiobookshelf reads EPUB, PDF, comics (CBR and CBZ), AZW3 and MOBI, holds ebook-only books, and can email a book to a Kindle or another e-reader. AudioSilo 2.5.0 reads one EPUB that sits beside an audiobook and keeps your place across devices: in the web player, and in the apps from app 2.5.0 (the Android APK today, the App Store and Google Play once their review clears); a folder with only an ebook in it isn’t a book to AudioSilo, and PDFs and comics aren’t read at all. Getting ebooks onto e-readers (an OPDS catalogue and Send to Kindle) is on our roadmap.
  • Changing your files from the browser. Audiobookshelf can upload books, write metadata and covers into the audio files, merge a book’s files into one M4B and edit chapter times. AudioSilo never writes to your library, by design. Uploads, an inbox folder and renaming and retagging files are on our roadmap for the Bridge, the optional companion container that is allowed to write. A chapter-time editor is on our roadmap too. Merging into M4B isn’t: a book made of many files already plays as one timeline in AudioSilo.
  • Podcasts with nothing extra to run. Audiobookshelf downloads podcasts itself, its app downloads episodes for offline listening, and it can notify you about new episodes and failing feeds. AudioSilo’s podcasts need its Bridge container to fetch the feeds, and episodes stream from your server; downloading episodes in the apps and podcast notifications are both on our roadmap.
  • Single sign-on. Audiobookshelf signs in with OpenID Connect (Authentik, Authelia, Keycloak and the like). That’s on our roadmap.
  • Metadata from many sources. Audiobookshelf matches against Audible, Google Books, iTunes, Open Library and others, and takes custom metadata providers. AudioSilo matches against its own community database only; custom providers are on our roadmap. (AudioSilo’s database is one of Audiobookshelf’s community providers, so you can use it there today.)
  • Sharing outward. Audiobookshelf can open an RSS feed for a book, series or podcast, and make a public share link for a book that expires. AudioSilo has neither; a time-limited guest link for one book is on our roadmap.
  • Playlists. Audiobookshelf has per-person playlists that mix books and podcast episodes. AudioSilo has collections and an Up next queue, but no playlists.
  • Noticing new files on its own. Audiobookshelf watches the library folder. AudioSilo doesn’t: it rescans when it starts, on a schedule you set and whenever you ask, and a new book folder someone opens in Folders before the next scan plays anyway.
  • Third-party apps and languages. Many apps talk to an Audiobookshelf server, so if you don’t like the official one you can pick another. AudioSilo works only with its own apps. Audiobookshelf’s web app is also translated into over 40 languages; AudioSilo’s player into a handful, and not every screen yet.

What AudioSilo does that Audiobookshelf doesn’t

  • Phone apps in both stores. The AudioSilo apps for iPhone, iPad and Android are released apps in the App Store and Google Play, not betas. The iPhone app has CarPlay. Android Auto and casting came to Android in app 2.1, which Google Play doesn’t carry yet (it still has 2.0.0), so on Android they need the APK from GitHub for now; Android Auto lists an app installed that way after one developer setting. App 2.5.0 is in review in both stores. Audiobookshelf’s own iOS app is a TestFlight beta that is currently full, and has no CarPlay (some third-party Audiobookshelf apps do).
  • Read-only by design. AudioSilo treats your folders as the source of truth and never writes to them, so you can mount the library read-only. Your edits, matches and custom covers live in its database, and progress and bookmarks follow a book when you move or rename its folder.
  • Spoiler-safe recaps and characters. For books in the community database, the player shows who’s who and the story so far, only up to where you are in the book. With the optional Bridge, your server can write them for a book the community hasn’t covered yet.
  • A household on one server. Household members (add a child or a partner without an invite), profiles and kids mode on a shared device with an optional PIN, and an opt-in “listening in the house” row.
  • A player built around the whole book. Time left at your speed and when you’ll finish, a timeline of the whole book with its chapters and bookmarks, a “Back to” chip that undoes an accidental jump, a sleep timer that fades out and marks where you dozed off, an Up next queue, a journal of bookmarks and notes you can export, and a shareable Year in listening.
  • An admin console that shows what needs fixing. Health lists unreadable files, duplicates, missing covers and unmatched books, with a bulk Match automatically; Activity shows who listened, when and to what; and there’s an audit log, notifications (webhook, ntfy, Discord) and a guided first-run setup.
  • A way in from Audiobookshelf. AudioSilo reads the metadata.json files Audiobookshelf leaves beside your books and imports everyone’s listening history, progress, finished dates and bookmarks. Moving from Audiobookshelf has the steps.
  • A smaller footprint, which brings us to the numbers.

The benchmark

We ran both servers on the same machine, against the same read-only libraries, with each one’s out-of-the-box settings, one at a time.

What we measured on

  • Server: an Intel Core i3-N305 (8 cores) with 16 GB of RAM: a low-power 15 W chip of the kind found in mini PCs and small NAS boxes, not a desktop or a data-centre machine. It runs Unraid (Linux 6.1, Docker 24). The libraries sit on the Unraid array, a hard disk reached through Unraid’s /mnt/user FUSE layer (shfs), the usual place for media on Unraid. Each app’s own data went on the cache pool.
  • Client: a Mac on the same home network, for the API and web timings. The API timings were also repeated on the server itself, over loopback, to take the network out.
  • Software: Audiobookshelf 2.37.2 (ghcr.io/advplyr/audiobookshelf, the current release) and AudioSilo from its :edge image of 28 October 2026, commit e465ccb, the commit tagged as AudioSilo 2.5.0 later that day (the :edge build reports its version as dev-e465ccb). Both images were pinned by digest for the whole run; the digests are in each tier’s meta.json.
  • Settings: each app’s defaults, as a new user would get them. The only change was serving AudioSilo over plain HTTP like Audiobookshelf (AUDIOSILO_TLS_MODE=off). Both containers mounted the library read-only.
  • Quiet host: the harness ran one app at a time (Audiobookshelf first, then AudioSilo, never both at once), wiped the app’s data folder before every first scan, dropped the Linux page cache before every cold measurement, checked that the library wasn’t changing, and recorded the load average around every phase. Our other containers on that server (18 of them) were running but idle.

Three libraries of LibriVox recordings, which are public domain, so anyone can build the same ones. None of them changed during the runs.

AudioSilo finished its first scan 25 to 40% sooner but used 9 to 37% more CPU time, because it reads up to eight files at once (more on that below). Audiobookshelf is the more frugal scanner. On the 1,000-book libraries the difference was about a minute; on the 5,000-book library AudioSilo’s first scan took 16 minutes and Audiobookshelf’s 22. A rescan with nothing changed took AudioSilo 0.5 to 1.6 seconds on the 1,000-book libraries, against 3.6 to 5.4 seconds for Audiobookshelf. Two harness slips sit in these times, both found after the run: every warm-cache rescan includes about a second the harness spent reading CPU time before it stopped the clock, for both apps, and every Audiobookshelf scan time includes about half a second it spent reading Audiobookshelf’s log. By the apps’ own clocks, the warm-cache rescans took 3.5 / 0.4 s, 2.2 / 0.3 s and 18.7 / 2.2 s. The harness published below has both fixed.

Everything here is fast for both: a person can’t tell 1 ms from 2 ms. The biggest gap is under full load: with 20 clients asking for the library’s first page as fast as they could from the Mac, AudioSilo answered 6 to 7 times as many requests a second as Audiobookshelf on every library, on the same low-power server. Two things temper that: AudioSilo answers a repeated request from a saved copy, and run from the server itself the gap was about 2 times (see What the numbers don’t show). What also shows is how the gap grows with the size of the library: at 5,000 books Audiobookshelf’s book page took about twice as long as at 1,000 and its play start 50 to 73% longer, while AudioSilo’s stayed flat. Audiobookshelf was quicker to send a cover it had already resized on both 1,000-book libraries, and on all three when timed on the server itself. AudioSilo’s web player doesn’t page through the library as Audiobookshelf’s does: it loads it all, in pages of 200, which took 6.5 to 7.6 ms for 1,000 books and 29 ms for 4,949.

This one compares different things. Audiobookshelf has no health page, so the nearest equivalent is its library filtered to items with issues: one query, which came back empty because Audiobookshelf leaves the damaged books out. AudioSilo’s Health page counts ten kinds of issue over the whole library (unreadable files, duplicates, missing covers, unmatched books and more) and makes eight requests to Audiobookshelf’s five. Audiobookshelf’s one query was a little quicker at 5,000 books; AudioSilo’s answer comes from a cache that is dropped at the next change (see below).

What the numbers don’t show

  • AudioSilo scans several files at once. It reads one audio file per CPU core at a time, up to eight. On a NAS with two cores it will use two; on a slow disk or a busy network share you can set scan.concurrency lower (Rescanning in the docs).
  • AudioSilo keeps list and search answers ready until something changes. Book lists, the recent shelf and search answers are saved until the next change to its database, and saving someone’s progress counts as a change. The benchmark repeats the same request, the saturating-load test included, so it mostly measures these saved answers. In a household where people are listening they are thrown away often, and many requests are worked out from scratch. Worked out from scratch, a library page is no faster than before we added this, in our own test of the server code (a generated 5,000-book library on a Mac, not the benchmark server). The console’s Health summary is saved the same way.
  • Where the load comes from changes the saturating result. From the Mac, AudioSilo answered six to seven times as many requests a second. Run from the server itself, where the load generator competes with the app for the same 8 cores, it was about twice as many: 1,592 / 2,971 on the 1,000-book mixed library, 1,448 / 2,961 on mostly M4B and 1,593 / 2,871 at 5,000.
  • The covers flatter both apps. The cover.jpg files our download script fetched are archive.org’s 180x180 pixel thumbnails, smaller than either app’s thumbnails, so there’s almost no resizing to do. Real audiobook art (500 to 2,400 pixels) costs both apps more on a first request. Treat the cover numbers as a lower bound.
  • AudioSilo rescans every library when it starts. The cold-start time stops at its first answer, which comes while that rescan is still running in the background.
  • The two apps don’t do identical work. Audiobookshelf loads its library 36 books at a time as you scroll; AudioSilo’s web player loads all of it and sorts it in the browser. Audiobookshelf’s home is one request; AudioSilo’s is several in parallel. Searching for an author’s name gives Audiobookshelf an author entry and AudioSilo that author’s books. Starting playback creates a session in Audiobookshelf and reads the saved place in AudioSilo. Each row in the harness results records what the answer contained.
  • A few LibriVox files are damaged at the source. Audiobookshelf leaves those books out of the library; AudioSilo lists them, with a note in Health that their files couldn’t be read. Neither can play them, so the book counts differ by one or two.
  • AudioSilo’s web player starts from your last visit. It shows Home and the library as you last saw them, then refreshes them, which helps its warm numbers. The cold runs start from an empty browser, so they get no such help.
  • Web timings depend on the client. JavaScript runs on the Mac, so a slower laptop or a phone takes longer in both.
  • One machine. This is one 8-core server with the library on a hard disk. A two-core NAS, an ARM board or a library on a network share will give different numbers, and could change who wins a close row.

Rerun it yourself

The harness and every result file are on this site: benchmarks/2026-10-audiobookshelf. It’s Python (standard library only) and Node with Playwright on the client, and Docker and SSH on the server. The README there has the protocol and every caveat in full, and each tier’s summary.md has all the numbers, including the ones too detailed for this post (p95 and maximum times, response sizes, the requests from the server’s own loopback, and the exact URLs). If you run it on your own hardware, we’d like to hear what you get.

Which should you choose?

Choose Audiobookshelf if you want one server for audiobooks, podcasts and an ebook library in many formats, if you want to upload books and edit their tags and chapters from the browser, if you need single sign-on today, or if you’d like to pick from the many third-party apps.

Choose AudioSilo if you want your folders left exactly as they are, released phone apps with CarPlay (and Android Auto in the Android APK until Google Play catches up), a player and console built around a household listening to whole books, spoiler-safe recaps, or a server that stays small and quick on low-power hardware like ours.

You don’t have to decide in one go. Both can run side by side on the same folders, because AudioSilo only reads them: Moving from Audiobookshelf to AudioSilo walks through it, from reading Audiobookshelf’s metadata.json to importing everyone’s listening history. If you’d rather stay with Audiobookshelf, its metadata can still come from AudioSilo’s community database.
